How far can a microphone hear a drone?

Two things take level out of the sound on its way to a microphone. Spreading thins it out over a growing sphere. The air turns some of it into heat. Move the sliders to see which one sets the range and what that does to the number of microphones a site needs.

Predict first

With the default settings, spreading alone lets the microphone hear the drone out to 1,000 m. Now move the analysis band from 1 kHz to 4 kHz, where the air absorbs about five times more. What range do you expect?

The sum behind it

The level reaching the microphone is the level 1 m from the drone, minus the transmission loss:

L(r)=L1− 20log10r⏟spreading − α(f)r⏟air

with r in metres. Spreading loses no energy; it only spreads the same power over a sphere of area 4πr2, so the level falls 6 dB each time the range doubles. Absorption grows in proportion to range, so far enough out it always wins. The two fall at the same rate at the crossover range

r×= 8.7α

with α in dB per metre. Inside it, the range follows the spreading law. Beyond it, every extra metre costs more than the last. The microphone hears the drone out to the range where L(r) falls to the level the node needs.

Each microphone covers an area that grows as r2, so the number needed for a site goes as

N∝1r2

A range that is 30% shorter needs about twice as many microphones. That is why a few dB of air absorption matter.

What this leaves out

  • Open air only. No ground reflection, buildings, wind or temperature gradients. Each can add or remove several dB at a few hundred metres.
  • One band at a time. A real drone spreads its sound over many bands. The range is set by whichever band reaches furthest above the noise there.
  • The level the node needs is just a number here. In practice the background noise at the site sets it, and so does how long the node listens. That is the next piece.
  • Pure-tone absorption. ISO 9613-1 gives absorption for a single tone. For a wide band it is an approximation.
